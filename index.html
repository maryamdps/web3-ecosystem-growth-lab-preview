FOUNDATION / THE TECHNOLOGY

First, understand what is happening underneath.

A blockchain is a shared network that records and verifies transactions. Web3 products sit on top of this infrastructure. Understanding the layers helps explain fees, speed, security, user experience and the trade-offs a product team must communicate.

THE STACK

From base network to the app someone actually uses.

The user sees a wallet and an application. Behind them are networks, nodes, validators, smart contracts and transaction rules.

HOW A TRANSACTION WORKS

What happens when someone presses “Send”?

A simple transaction lifecycle. Exact implementation details vary by wallet, network and application, but these are the core steps users and marketers need to understand.

01 / WALLET

Sign

The user reviews the destination, asset, amount and fee, then signs with their wallet.

02 / NETWORK

Broadcast

The signed transaction is sent to a node and propagated across the network.

03 / EXECUTION

Process

The network checks validity and executes the transaction or smart-contract call.

04 / CONSENSUS

Confirm

Validators propose and attest to blocks under Proof of Stake; the chain progresses towards finality.

Gas is not just a random fee

Gas measures the computational work required to process an on-chain action. On Ethereum, the total fee is influenced by gas used and the effective gas price. The base fee changes with network demand; users may add a priority fee to incentivise faster inclusion.

Why smart contracts matter

A smart contract is code deployed on a blockchain. Applications call these contracts to perform actions such as swaps, lending, token transfers or membership checks. The user is interacting with software whose rules execute on-chain.

WHY LAYER 2 EXISTS

Scaling is a product and growth issue — not only a technical one.

When a base network is congested, transaction costs and confirmation experience can become barriers. Rollups execute transactions outside Ethereum's Layer 1 environment and post data or proofs back to Ethereum according to their design.

What an L2 can improve

  • Lower transaction costs for many common actions.
  • Higher throughput and a smoother app experience.
  • More room for consumer apps, games and frequent interactions.
  • More affordable experimentation for users and developers.

What users still need to understand

  • Which network their assets are on.
  • Whether they need a bridge and how to use it safely.
  • Which token pays transaction fees on that network.
  • How withdrawals, settlement and security assumptions differ.

Important distinction: “Layer 2” is not one identical technology. Optimistic and zero-knowledge rollups use different proving and dispute/verification mechanisms. Always explain the specific network, rather than treating every L2 as interchangeable.

PUT THE TECHNOLOGY INTO CONTEXT

Example: a freelancer receiving an international payment.

This is the kind of real-world problem that can make blockchain infrastructure relevant. The person is not waking up wanting an L1 or an L2. They want to receive money, understand the cost and access it reliably.

The user's job to be done

“I need to get paid by an overseas client without losing too much to fees or waiting days.”

CLIENTSends paymentChooses asset and network
NETWORKProcesses transferFee and confirmation matter
FREELANCERReceives fundsNeeds access and conversion

What the growth marketer must understand

The complete journey, not just the transaction

  • How the sender acquires and sends the stablecoin.
  • Whether sender and recipient use the same network.
  • Wallet setup, recovery and transaction-signing confidence.
  • Network fees, transfer confirmation and failed-transaction support.
  • How the recipient converts to local currency and cashes out.
  • Local availability, compliance and provider restrictions.

Marketing implication

Lead with the outcome: “Receive international payments with a clear view of fees and transfer status.” Explain stablecoins, wallets and networks only when they help the person make a confident decision.

Research questions to validate

Which corridors are supported? What are the full sender and recipient costs? How long does each step take? What cash-out options exist? What happens when a payment is sent on the wrong network? These must be verified for the specific product and market.

01 / RESEARCH

Start with people, use cases and product reality.

A useful ecosystem assessment combines infrastructure research, product walkthroughs, competitor context and audience needs. The segments below are working hypotheses for a payments-focused product — not claims about measured market size.

01AUDIENCE

Cross-border money movers

People who regularly send or receive money internationally: freelancers, remote workers, families and small businesses.

Need: predictable fees, reliable delivery, easy cash-out and trust.
02AUDIENCE

Global freelancers & creators

Independent workers paid by clients or platforms in other countries, often juggling currencies and payment providers.

Need: get paid easily, track incoming funds and convert when needed.
03AUDIENCE

Crypto-curious first-timers

People who have heard of stablecoins but do not yet understand wallets, networks, custody or transaction risk.

Need: plain-language education, guided setup and reassurance.
04AUDIENCE

Active on-chain users

People already using wallets, DeFi or L2 apps who compare speed, fees, liquidity and network support.

Need: dependable infrastructure, useful integrations and transparent trade-offs.
05AUDIENCE

Developers & product teams

Builders choosing where to deploy, which users to serve and which infrastructure or ecosystem tools to integrate.

Need: documentation, developer support, distribution and a credible user base.

Product and competitor audit

Compare wallet setup, supported networks, stablecoin availability, transaction steps, fee visibility, help content and cash-out paths.

Output: journey map, competitor matrix and prioritised friction points.

02 / GROWTH OPPORTUNITY

Find the gap between interest and first useful value.

The opportunity is not simply “get more people to connect a wallet”. It is to help the right user complete a relevant task safely, understand what happened and have a reason to return.

Potential friction points to investigate

  • People do not understand why they need a wallet.
  • Network choice, gas fees or token requirements appear too early.
  • Users cannot tell whether a transfer is pending, complete or failed.
  • Sending and receiving networks do not match.
  • Cash-out options are unclear or unavailable in the user's market.
  • Education focuses on crypto terminology instead of the task the user wants to complete.

How to validate before investing

  • Interview 8–12 people across the priority segments.
  • Observe 5–8 first-time users completing the target journey.
  • Review product analytics from landing page to successful transfer.
  • Compare end-to-end costs and completion times across alternatives.
  • Tag support questions and failed or abandoned steps.

These are proposed research methods and sample sizes, not completed interviews or findings.

Acquisition gap

Generic crypto messaging attracts curiosity but may not reach people with a concrete payment need.

Activation gap

Wallet creation is an intermediate step. The meaningful event is a successfully completed, understood payment.

Retention gap

A one-off transfer does not guarantee habit. Repeat value may come from recurring invoices, payment reminders or saved recipients.
